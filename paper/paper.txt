Abstract
An irregularly sampled time series reflects not only the underly-
ing dynamics of a system, but also the process through which it
is observed. Anobservation processdetermines when and which
variables are recorded, yet models for irregular time series are
evaluated almost exclusively under the observation process of the
training benchmark. This leaves robustness to changes in the obser-
vation process largely unmeasured. We introduceOPERA, a training
framework for robustness to observation-process shift. OPERA com-
bines observation-process augmentation, which exposes the model
to diverse observation processes during training, withprivileged
observation self-distillation, in which a teacher receives a richer
observation of the same history and provides task-level supervi-
sion to a student restricted to the deployment observation. Both
mechanisms act on the observation process alone: the architecture,
the parameter count and the inference path are unchanged, and
the teacher and the richer observation are discarded after training.

1 Introduction
Temporal data reflect both how a system evolves and how that
system is measured. For example, a patient’s physiological state
changes continuously, but the recorded measurements depend on
when clinicians choose to order laboratory tests. The first process
describes the underlying trajectory, while the second determines
which parts of that trajectory become available to a model.
Irregular time-series modelshave largely focused on represent-
ing the observations that are available. Existing approaches use
recurrent models with decay [4], continuous-time latent dynamics
[6, 13, 24], set and attention-based aggregation [ 12, 29], graph-
guided representations [39, 40], and query-based embeddings that

Classical missing data theory formally characterizes how obser-
vations are omitted through mechanisms such as Missing Com-
pletely At Random or Missing At Random [?]. While a rich lit-
erature addresses representation learning under missingness [?],
these frameworks typically assume the underlying missingness
mechanism itself remains stationary across datasets. We study a
distinct and overlooked source of distribution shift, which we call
observation-process shift. In this setting, the underlying distribution
of the system trajectory and the prediction target remain perfectly
stationary, but the missingness mechanism governing data avail-
ability changes between training and deployment. For example, a
hospital may alter its measurement schedule or adopt new admin-
istrative protocols while the patient’s physiological dynamics and
clinical risk remain unchanged. The model therefore faces a funda-
mentally different observation process even though the underlying
prediction problem is identical. Standard train–test splits do not
reveal this failure mode because both splits are generated under the
original observation process, leaving models entirely unprepared
for structural changes in data availability at deployment.
We make this shift measurable by re-observing each held-out
history through a different admissible observation process while
keeping its prediction target unchanged. Figure 1 illustrates this
mechanism using a patient history from the PhysioNet dataset [30].
By systematically withholding recorded measurements according to
distinct processes—such as random dropout, temporal subsampling,
or channel removal—the model receives radically different inputs
even though the underlying physiological trajectory is fixed. This
isolates the effect of observation-process shift from standard co-
variate shift. This evaluation reveals that the resulting performance
degradation is substantial and highly variable. More importantly,
the severity of the shift cannot be explained by data sparsity alone.
Processes that remove approximately the same fraction of observa-
tions produce drastically different errors depending on the structure
of the missingness. As Figure 1 demonstrates, two processes can
leave the exact same number of measurements for a target channel,
yet yield a doubled prediction error simply because one process
removes critical correlating information from other variables. The
most damaging type of observation loss is therefore deeply tied to
both cross-channel dependencies and the underlying data modal-
ity. These findings emphasize that robustness must be evaluated
against the specific structure of the observation process rather than
a global scalar measure of observation density.

correlations within the augmented missingness patterns just to min-
imize training error. To prevent this, Phase 1 creates the necessary
environmental diversity, setting the stage forprivileged observation

exploits an information advantage available only during training.

uncorrupted observation process. A teacher network receives this
dense view, while a student receives the augmented, sparse views
generated in Phase 1. By training the student to match the teacher’s
task-level predictive distribution—in addition to the ground-truth
target—the teacher effectively filters the signal. It passes on soft
knowledge about what can and cannot be reliably predicted from
the underlying trajectory, preventing the student from memorizing
the artifacts of its restricted observation window. Because the stu-
dent learns to approximate the teacher’s generalized understanding
rather than overfitting to the noise of the sparse data, it develops a
much more robust representation of the underlying dynamics. The
richer observation and teacher are used exclusively during training
and are discarded at inference.

2 Related Work
2.1 Irregular time-series representations and
the shared-observation assumption.
Deep learning for irregular multivariate time series spans several
architectural paradigms. Interpolation-based approaches address ir-
regular sampling by mapping observations collected at non-uniform
time intervals onto a regular time grid or a set of reference points
[28]. Continuous-time models maintain latent states governed by
differential equations [6, 13, 24]. The set and attention-based archi-
tectures treat the observations as unordered tuples of time, channel,

and value [12, 29].Graph-based models address asynchronous ob-
servations across variables by representing channels as nodes and
propagating information along learned inter-channel dependencies
[39, 40], and image-based methods sidestep irregular sampling by
rendering each variable as a line plot, turning unaligned observa-
tions into an image that a pretrained vision backbone can process
without imputation [16]. More recently, query-based tokenization
aggregates irregular observations into discrete tokens, allowing
standard multivariate backbones [19, 23] to consume irregular in-
put directly [17]. These paradigms differ in how they represent the
available observations, but are trained and evaluated in the same
way, training and test splits are drawn from a single dataset and,
therefore, share its observation process. Our work does not intro-
duce another encoder architecture. It intervenes in the observation
process seen during training and applies unchanged to any of the
encoders above.
2.2 Evaluating robustness to missing
observations.
When robustness to missing observations is assessed, it is mea-
sured at test time only and follows one of two protocols. The first is
leave-sensors-out, introduced with Raindrop [40] a fixed or random
subset of variables is hidden in the validation and test sets while
the training set is left unchanged [14, 16, 40]. The second sweeps a
scalar missingness rate by randomly dropping observations or time
points [14, 15]. Both protocols summarize the observation process
by a single quantity, the fraction of variables or observations re-
moved, and neither is used during training. Moreover, some public
implementations of leave-sensors-out only zero out the values of
removed variables and keep their observation indicators and times-
tamps. The model therefore still sees these variables as recorded,
only at their mean value, instead of as missing. Such evaluations
measure robustness to value imputation rather than to a change
in the observation process. Our protocol instead re-observes each
held-out history by element-wise removal, so that withheld obser-
vations are indistinguishable from observations that were never
recorded, and it organizes the resulting processes along density,
timing, and variable availability, with leave-sensors-out as a special
case of variable dropout, separated into families seen and unseen
during training.
2.3 Observation-process shift and robust
training.
In clinical time series, missingness itself carries predictive signal
[4, 18]. When and how often laboratory tests are ordered can pre-
dict outcomes better than their results [1], and irregular time-series
models rely on the timing and structure of observations rather than
on values alone [22]. Models that exploit this signal become exposed
to changes in record-keeping. Domain adaptation under missing-
ness shift shows that this setting reduces to covariate shift when
missingness indicators are available [41], and later work extends
it to data missing not at random [32]. These works consider static
covariates rather than processes acting jointly on timestamps and
channels, and they assume access to target-domain data, whereas
we assume no access to the deployment process.
Without target data, a natural alternative is to train on synthetic
variation [34, 36] and to concentrate on high-error regimes [21, 25–
27]. Close to our setting, ATENet [14] masks random time points
of irregular series and enforces contrastive consistency between
representations of the full and masked views, using masking as a
regularizer under the benchmark’s own observation process. Opera
instead samples realizable observation processes to simulate deploy-
ment shift, and Opera++ can be read as a distributionally robust
objective over a constrained set of them. Unlike representation-level
constraints [2, 3, 5, 9], which require anti-collapse mechanisms and
can over-constrain the model, Opera supervises predictions asym-
metrically, from the richer view to the sparser one (Section??).
2.4 Privileged information and asymmetric
self-distillation.
Learning using privileged information formalizes the use of auxil-
iary inputs available only during training [35], and knowledge dis-
tillation provides a natural mechanism for exploiting them [11, 20].
Standard distillation transfers knowledge from a larger teacher, and
generalized distillation from a teacher trained on privileged features
that the student never sees. Self-distillation removes the capacity
gap and shows that a student with the teacher’s own architecture
can match or exceed it [7, 38]. Opera combines the last two ideas.
The teacher shares the student’s architecture and task objective,
and the privileged information is a richer observation of the same
history rather than an extra feature or a larger model. Because both
views are sub-samples of the same recording, this privileged signal
requires no external annotations, auxiliary sensors, or additional
data, and the teacher never sees a target or a future state denied to
the student. The teacher and the richer view are used only during
training and are discarded at inference.
Teacher–student training on differently corrupted views is also
common in semi-supervised learning. Mean Teacher [33] and Fix-
Match [31] enforce agreement between predictions on weakly and
strongly augmented inputs, and Noisy Student [37] trains a noised
student on labels from a clean teacher. Cross-modal and modality
distillation transfer knowledge from a modality available at training
time to a model that must work without it [8, 10]. These methods
use augmentation or missing modalities to exploit unlabeled data or
to compensate for an absent input stream. Opera targets a different
failure. Its corruptions are realizable observation processes rather
than generic perturbations, its supervised setting keeps ground-
truth labels alongside the teacher, and its teacher signal reduces the
variance of the training target on views too sparse to determine the
label, so that the student is not pushed to fit noise through artificial
missingness patterns.

3.2 Three structural dimensions of an
observation process
An observation process cannot be adequately characterized by
a single scalar missingness parameter. Because S operates on a
binary mask indexed over both time and variable spaces, it can
diverge across three distinct structural dimensions—density,timing,
andvariable availability. We design both our observation process
taxonomy and our evaluation suite around these three axes to
enable precise attribution of model performance degradation.
We enforce that all constructed processes arerealizable. Each
process must represent a physically plausible sampling protocol
applied to the true underlying trajectory, guaranteeing that the
ground-truth prediction target remains well-defined. We restrict
our consideration to processes that act on the observation mask
strictly viaelement-wise removal,
S:(𝑡,𝑚) ↦→ (𝑡,𝑚⊙𝜅), 𝜅∈{0,1} 𝐾×𝑁,(4)

cially imputed. Consequently, every realization S(𝑋) is a strict
sub-sample of the original recording. This property ensures no syn-
thetic feature values are injected into the input stream, allowing
a single held-out evaluation trajectory to be re-observed across
multiple shifted conditions without altering its ground-truth target.

Non-separability across variables.A common pitfall is attempting

analyzed as structured, multivariate entities.

4 The Opera Framework
The core principle of our approach is to intervene strictly on the
data-generating mechanism rather than the model architecture. Ev-
erything in this section acts on the observation processS (Figure 2).
The model architecture, its parameter count, and its deployment in-
ference path remain entirely unmodified. We first detail privileged
observation self-distillation, which forms the theoretical core of the
method, followed by the observation-process augmentation that
supports it.
4.1 Rich and sparse observations of a stationary
history
At deployment, a model is bound by the prevailing observation
mechanism and must make predictions using whatever sparse data
it receives. During training, however, we can manipulate the miss-
ingness mechanism. Crucially, the same underlying history can
be observedmore richlythan deployment conditions permit. We
define these two views as
O𝑠 =S𝑠(𝑋),O 𝑟 =S𝑟(𝑋),(6)

information in the sense of [ 35]. Distillation [ 11, 20] provides a
mathematically principled mechanism to transfer this privileged
structural knowledge into the deployment-restricted student.

what can be robustly predicted despite the restrictive deployment
mechanism.
Self-distillation through data asymmetry.The asymmetry be-
tween these two networks originates entirely in data space. The
teacher is not a larger parameterization or a distinct expert net-
work. It is the exact same predictive family evaluated on a richer
view of the same sample. While teacher-student distillation is a
standard technique, our specific contribution lies in thesourceof
the privileged signal. It is generated purely by evaluating the same
history under a different observation process, requiring no external
annotations, auxiliary sensors, or expanded datasets.
Preserving the anti-leakage invariant.To maintain a mathemati-
cally valid evaluation, the teacher and student must differonlyin
their observation density. Both networks operate over the same
historical window, target the same prediction times, and receive
the same supervision. The teacher never observes a future state
or a ground-truth label denied to the student. Without this strict
boundary, the distillation term would degenerate into label leakage.
We enforce this invariant through assertions over every batch key
that carries supervision, validating it programmatically for both
forecasting and classification tasks.
Inference constraints.At deployment, the student processes a
single sparse observation to emit a prediction. The teacher network,
the rich observation view, and the auxiliary forward pass are strictly
training-time constructs.

5 Experiments
Our claim is about the observation process, not about an architec-
ture, a task or a dataset. An experiment that supported it on one
encoder, one task and one dataset would not distinguish a property
of observation-process shift from a property of that particular setup.
We therefore vary two things that the claim should be independent
of, and hold the protocol fixed across both.
Axis 1: where the model sits.Irregular observations reach a model
in one of two ways, and we cover both.
• A conventional time-series model with an irregularity-
aware embedding.A query-based embedding [ 17] pools
the observations belonging to a variable or a temporal patch
into a fixed-size token, after which a standard multivari-
ate backbone consumes it unchanged. We run three such
backbones, differing in what they mix over: variables, time
patches, and time patches through an MLP. This is the plug-
in regime, where irregularity is handled before the model
and the model itself is ordinary.
• A purpose-built irregular time-series model.Architec-
tures designed for irregular data directly, which interleave
time, value and mask rather than pooling first. [name the
integrated model(s); tPatchGNN first, then one of GraFITi /
HyperIMTS]
The two regimes make different assumptions about where irregu-
larity is absorbed, so a mechanism that helps in both is acting on
the observation process rather than on either design.
Axis 2: what the model is asked to do.We coverforecasting
andclassification. The distinction matters here more than usual:
a forecaster must reconstruct the future trajectory of each vari-
able, so losing a variable removes the only direct evidence about it,
whereas a classifier pools the whole history into one decision and

Coverage: datasets.Across both axes we use [N] datasets span-

5.1 Benchmark protocol
One protocol is used for every cell of both axes, so numbers differ
only in the architecture, the task or the dataset.
Evaluation by re-observation.For each condition we replace
thehistoryof every held-out input by that observation process
applied to it, and leave the prediction times, forecast targets and
labels untouched (Section ??). The underlying trajectory and the
supervision are therefore held fixed by construction, and one test
set yields as many shifted conditions as there are processes. The
pseudo-random draw is seeded independently of the run, so every
architecture, arm and seed is scored on byte-identical shifted inputs
and comparisons are paired at the level of individual inputs.
Conditions.The clean condition is the dataset’s own observa-
tion process. Four shifted conditions each move one dimension of
Section ?? past what training covers, plus one that moves two at

Arms.[PLACEHOLDER — confirm the arm list.] Each archi-

5.2 Metrics
Forecasting uses masked MSE and MAE over observed target entries.
Binary classification uses AUROC and AUPRC; the multi-class
task uses accuracy and macro F1. We summarise sensitivity by the
relative degradation of a model against itsownclean score,
Δ= 1|E| ∑︁S∈E errS−err cleanerrclean ,(11)
over the shifted conditionsE. For classification we take err= 1−
AUROC (or1−accuracy ), so Δ is computed on the metric used to
judge the task and is lower-is-better for both tasks; cross-entropy
degradation can move opposite to AUROC and we do not use it.
Three cautions govern how Δ is read, and we observe them
throughout. It is normalised by a model’s own clean score, so a uni-
formly weaker model can post a flattering ratio; absolute clean and
shifted performance is therefore reported beside it everywhere. Its
magnitude is not comparable across metric families, since forecast-
ing error is unbounded above while a classifier can only degrade
to chance, which caps Δ at a value fixed by the metric; its sign
is comparable. And a model that has collapsed to chance cannot
degrade at all, so a near-zero Δ is checked against the clean column
before it is read as robustness.

of observation-process augmentation and privileged observation
self-distillation. Finally, we evaluate generalisation to unseen obser-
vation processes, study the additional benefit of hard observation-
process mining in OPERA++, and report the computational cost.

Limitations.Every number here is single-seed and preliminary.
The protocol calls for five paired seeds, and the smaller differences
in Tables??, ?? and ?? are within the range we would expect seeds to
move; the mining comparison in particular rests on a0.00006MSE

processes are synthetic re-observations of real records, which is
what makes the targets well defined but is weaker evidence than
a shift between two genuinely different acquisition environments.

record; we do not vary the richness of the teacher’s view, so we
cannot say how much of the distillation gain depends on the size
of the teacher–student observation gap.
8 Conclusion
Irregularly sampled data is the product of a temporal process and
an observation process, and the second can shift independently of
the first. We formalised that shift, gave a controlled protocol that
re-observes held-out histories while preserving their targets, and
found degradation ranging from a few percent to more than an order
of magnitude across seven task settings, in a way that observation
density alone does not explain. The method we propose exploits the
one asymmetry the setting offers: during training, and only during
training, the same history can be observed more richly than de-
ployment allows. Privileged observation self-distillation turns that
richer observation into a task-level target for a student restricted
to the deployment view, and it improves absolute accuracy where
the sparse view leaves the target ambiguous. Observation-process
augmentation supplies the student’s views and is what reduces sen-
sitivity to the shift itself; hard observation-process mining improves
worst-case error, but only when its candidate pool is harsher than
the mixture augmentation already draws, which is why we report
it as a conditional extension rather than a component. None of the
three changes what deployment requires: one sparse observation
and the student. The natural next questions are how the gain varies
with the size of the teacher–student observation gap, and whether
a shift between two real acquisition environments behaves like the
synthetic re-observations studied here.
